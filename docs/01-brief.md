# Brief

**Creative director and lead UX:** Noah
**Status:** v0 prototype complete (October 2026)
**Name:** Palit (approved 2026-10-16; the working name was Palette)

## The idea

Designers work across many apps, tabs, screens and files at once. The colors they notice along the way get lost: a hex pasted into a note, a screenshot on the desktop, a swatch left behind in some file. Palit sits quietly in the menu bar. Press a hotkey and steal a color from anything you're looking at. It's kept for good, along with where it came from.

Many ambient tools (Cluely, Granola, Wispr Flow) use AI to make work smoother, sometimes to the point where people stop doing the thing themselves. Palit goes the other way. It is a tool for **noticing**. Over months, the archive shows you your own taste: the reds you keep coming back to, the neutrals you lean on, the colors you only find in photographs.

## Who it's for

- Designers who move between Figma, the browser, references and photos all day.
- Anyone who works on a laptop and wants to get better at seeing color: students, developers, writers who make their own covers.

## What already exists (and why this is different)

Hotkey color picking is a solved problem, and Palit shouldn't pretend otherwise.

- **Sip** (Mac): a menu bar picker with a hotkey and an automatic color history. https://docs.sipapp.io/picker
- **ColorSlurp** (Mac): a hotkey picker with history, palettes grouped into projects, harmonies, contrast checks and iCloud sync. https://colorslurp.com/whats-new
- **Pick** (Ubuntu): saves a screenshot with each color "so you can remember where you got it from." This is the closest thing to Palit's idea. https://www.kryogenix.org/code/pick/

These are utilities: grab a color, paste it, forget it. Palit is a collection. What sets it apart:

1. **The archive is the product.** Every color is numbered, dated and never lost. The archive is something to browse, like a personal Pantone book or an Are.na channel.
2. **Every color keeps its source.** It records the app, window, URL, time, the colors around it, and optionally a crop. Color only makes sense in context (Josef Albers, *Interaction of Color*).
3. **It trains your eye instead of replacing it.** No auto-names and no generated palettes. Feedback describes what you kept and shows when you've kept something like it before.
4. **It's quiet.** One keystroke, a short confirmation, then nothing.

## Success looks like

- Capturing is fast enough to use without thinking: under a second from hotkey to kept.
- After a month, opening the archive tells you something true about your own taste.
- Designers say it changed how they look at things, not just how fast they copy hex codes.
